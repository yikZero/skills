# English AI Tells

Check content-level problems first (undue significance, vague attribution), then words, structures, and style. For each pattern: how to spot it, how to fix it. The general fix: if the sentence still works after deleting the phrase, delete it; if it loses information, replace it with the specific thing, not a synonym.

## Word Level

### 1. AI vocabulary

Words that spike in post-2023 text, especially in clusters:

additionally, align with, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, dive deep, elevate, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), holistic, interplay, intricate, landscape (abstract), leverage, meticulous, navigate (challenges), pivotal, robust, seamless, showcase, streamline, tapestry, testament, underscore, unleash, unlock, valuable, vibrant

**Fix:** Use the plain word: use, improve, show, important, detailed. One of these words is fine; three in a paragraph is a fingerprint.

### 2. Empty intensifiers and hedges

really, truly, actually, literally, genuinely, deeply, fundamentally, inherently, incredibly, arguably, notably, importantly, crucially

**Fix:** Cut intensifiers that add emphasis without information. Keep adverbs that change meaning (slowly, twice, upstream). The target is human frequency, not zero.

### 3. Throat-clearing openers

"Here's the thing:", "Let me be clear", "It turns out", "The truth is", "It's worth noting that", "Here's why that matters", "In today's fast-paced world", "At the end of the day", "When it comes to", "Can we talk about", "Let that sink in"

**Fix:** Delete the opener, state the point.

### 4. Business jargon and filler replacements

| Avoid | Use instead |
| --- | --- |
| navigate (challenges) | handle, deal with |
| unpack (an idea) | explain |
| lean into | accept, commit to |
| game-changer | (name the actual change) |
| deep dive | analysis, close look |
| double down | commit harder |
| moving forward | next, from now on |
| circle back | return to |
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| at this point in time | now |
| has the ability to | can |
| a wide range of | many (or count them) |
| plays a key role in | (use a real verb) |

### 5. Vague declaratives

"The implications are significant." "The reasons are structural." "The stakes are high."

**Fix:** Name the implication, the reason, the stake. If you can't, the sentence has no content; cut it.

## Structure Level

### 6. Binary contrast ("not X, but Y")

"Not because X, but because Y" / "The answer isn't X. It's Y." / "It's not just X, it's Y" / "stops being X and starts being Y"

The single most recognizable AI structure. **Fix:** State Y directly. Keep at most one per piece, and only where a real misconception needs correcting.

### 7. Negative listing

"It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z." **Fix:** State Z. The reader doesn't need the runway.

### 8. Rule of three

"seamless, intuitive, and powerful"; three parallel clauses; three-item lists everywhere. **Fix:** Two items, or one item explained properly. Lists of four or more earn their place only when every item carries information.

### 9. Superficial -ing analysis

Fact, then a participle phrase gluing on unearned significance: "...reflecting the community's deep connection to the land", "...highlighting the company's commitment to innovation".

**Fix:** End the sentence at the fact.

### 10. Copula avoidance

"serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "marks", "boasts", "features", "offers" in place of "is" and "has". **Fix:** "The gallery is LAAA's exhibition space. It has four rooms."

### 11. False agency

Inanimate things doing human verbs: "the complaint becomes a fix", "the decision emerges", "the data tells us", "the culture shifts". **Fix:** Name the person. "The team fixed it that week." If no specific person fits, use "you".

### 12. Passive voice

"Mistakes were made", "It is believed that". **Fix:** Find the actor, put them at the front.

### 13. Synonym cycling

the protagonist → the main character → the central figure → the hero. **Fix:** One thing, one name, the whole piece.

### 14. False ranges

"From the Big Bang to the cosmic web, from stellar birth to the dance of dark matter." **Fix:** "The book covers the Big Bang, star formation, and dark matter."

### 15. Rhetorical setups

"What if I told you...", "Here's what I mean:", "Think about it:", "The question isn't whether X, but how". **Fix:** Make the point. No drumroll.

### 16. Dramatic fragmentation

"Speed. Quality. Cost. Pick two. That's it. That's the tradeoff." **Fix:** Complete sentences; one fragment per piece at most.

### 17. Formulaic sections

"Despite these challenges... the future looks promising"; generic upbeat conclusions ("Exciting times lie ahead"). **Fix:** End with a concrete fact, a next step, or an open question. A flat ending beats an inflated one.

### 18. Fake precision

Sourceless exact numbers: "a 0.3-second pause", "47% faster". **Fix:** Cite the source if there is one; otherwise honest vagueness ("about twice as fast") or cut.

## Style Level

### 19. Em dashes

AI uses them at several times human frequency. **Fix:** Replace most with commas, periods, or colons. Keep one or two that earn a real pause.

### 20. Bold overuse and inline-header lists

Mechanical **key term** bolding; bullets shaped "**Topic:** sentence about topic." **Fix:** Bold only what a skimming reader must catch, and let list items state the point directly.

### 21. Title Case Headings, emoji bullets, curly-quote inconsistency

**Fix:** Sentence case headings. No emoji in article prose. Pick one quote style and keep it.

## Voice Level

### 22. Collaborative leftovers

"I hope this helps", "Certainly!", "Let me know if you'd like me to expand", "In this article, we'll explore". **Fix:** Delete. An article is not a chat reply.

### 23. Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers

"As of [date]", "While specific details are limited, it appears that...". **Fix:** Verify and state the fact, or say plainly that you couldn't find it.

### 24. Vague attribution

"Experts believe", "Industry reports show", "Observers note". **Fix:** Name the source (who, when, where), or own the claim yourself: "I think".

### 25. Lazy extremes

every, always, never, everyone, nobody doing vague work. **Fix:** Specifics. "Most teams I've worked with" beats "everyone".

## Content Level

### 26. Undue significance

"a pivotal moment", "marked a turning point", "left an indelible mark", "a testament to". **Fix:** Shrink the claim back to the size of the fact. Readers judge significance; adjectives don't.

### 27. Promotional tone

"breathtaking", "world-class", "redefines", "revolutionary", "nestled in the heart of". **Fix:** Verifiable facts. "The main camera has a one-inch sensor" beats "a groundbreaking camera experience".

## Rhythm Checks

- Three consecutive sentences the same length → break one.
- Every paragraph ends on a punchy one-liner → vary the endings.
- A question answered in the next breath → cut the question or let it breathe.
- Anything that sounds like a pull-quote → rewrite it plainer.

## Example

**Before:**
> Here's the thing: building products is hard. Not because the technology is complex. Because people are complex. In today's fast-paced landscape, teams must navigate uncertainty while fostering alignment — a testament to the enduring challenge of collaboration. Let that sink in.

**After:**
> Building products is hard. The technology is manageable; getting fifteen people to agree on what to build is not. Last quarter we shipped two weeks late because two senior engineers disagreed about the queue design and nobody forced a decision.

(The "last quarter" detail is allowed only because the author supplied it. Never invent one.)
