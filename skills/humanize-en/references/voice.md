# Where the Human Voice Comes From

Removing bad patterns is half the job. Sterile, voiceless "clean" text is as obviously machine-made as text full of tells. The other half of editing is putting a specific person back into the draft: someone with a position, things they know, a way of talking. This file covers what to inject — always inside the Honesty Lines in SKILL.md, which override everything here.

## Find the claim first

The first editing move is not hunting for tells. It is restating the draft's claim in one sentence, then testing it: **would anyone disagree?**

- "Efficiency matters for teams" — nobody disagrees, so it's not a claim, it's filler.
- "Daily standups are a net negative for teams under ten people" — people will disagree. That's an article.

If you can't find the sentence, that's a content problem; surface de-flavoring won't save it. Go back and ask the author what they're trying to say. If the claim exists but is buried under "on the one hand... on the other hand", the rewrite's main job is digging it out and putting it up front.

The viewpoint also lives mid-piece: after each fact, where is the author's reaction? Where it's been flattened into neutral summary, put it back. "I honestly don't know what to make of this" is more human than any balanced list of pros and cons.

## The specificity hierarchy

When a rewrite needs detail, draw only from these four sources, in order of trust:

1. The author's own experience (explicitly provided)
2. Author-supplied material (interviews, docs, chat logs)
3. Verifiable public facts (with a citable source)
4. Honest vagueness ("about half", "sometime last year, I think")

There is no fifth source. Fabricated specificity — fake anecdotes, fake numbers, fake quotes — is the worst possible failure. Stay abstract before you invent.

The reverse matters just as much: **the draft's existing details are its most valuable parts.** The most common editing accident is deleting the only real number, date, or example as clutter and replacing it with a prettier empty sentence. Cut words, never facts.

Show, don't tell:

- Telling: "This is unsettling."
- Showing: "At 3 a.m., with nobody watching, the agents were still running. That's what unsettles me."

The first is a conclusion; the second lets the reader reach it. Material for showing comes from the four sources above.

## Rhythm

Short sentence. Then a long one that takes its time, walks through cause and effect, and trusts the reader to stay with it. Alternate, but not on a schedule — regular alternation is just another metronome.

- Vary paragraph length: a one-sentence paragraph is allowed, once per page.
- Vary paragraph endings: not every paragraph gets to end on a clipped assertion.
- Stop with periods. Don't manufacture breath with em dashes and ellipses.

There is one test: read it aloud. Wherever you stumble, run out of air, or hear a news anchor, that's the edit.

## First person and feeling

"I" isn't unprofessional; it's honest — it marks the boundary between judgment and fact. "I keep coming back to..." and "What bothers me is..." beat "It is worth reflecting that..." every time.

Admit mixed feelings. Real reactions are rarely pure: "impressive, and a little unsettling" is more credible than either praise or criticism alone. When admitting uncertainty, be specific about it: not "it remains to be seen" but "I haven't seen this work in a team larger than ten."

Humor and edge aren't required, but if the draft has them, don't sand them off. Same for the author's quirks: pet phrases, odd metaphors, regionalisms are the piece's fingerprint, not defects.

## Rough edges

Perfect structure feels algorithmic. Real thinking leaves marks:

- A one-sentence digression that comes back.
- Self-correction: "Slow isn't quite right — unstable."
- A question the author never resolved, left standing instead of forced closed.

Keep these where the draft has them. But the edges must be real: hesitation performed to seem authentic is a deeper fake (see Honesty Lines).

## Full example

**Before (pattern-free, but nobody home):**

> The experiment produced interesting results. The agents generated three million lines of code. Some developers were impressed, while others remained skeptical. The implications remain unclear.

No sentence is wrong. There's also no claim, no reaction, and a metronome rhythm — it reads like meeting minutes.

**After (someone's home):**

> I honestly don't know what to make of this. Three million lines of code, generated while the humans were presumably asleep. Half the developer community lost its mind; the other half is busy explaining why it doesn't count. The truth is probably somewhere boring in the middle, but I keep thinking about those agents running through the night.

What changed: a first-person position, reactions to the facts, mixed sentence lengths, and uncertainty that lands somewhere concrete ("agents running through the night") instead of "the implications remain unclear."
